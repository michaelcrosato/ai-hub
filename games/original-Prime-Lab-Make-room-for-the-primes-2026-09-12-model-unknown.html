Behind the pattern

The sieve of Eratosthenes

A sieve keeps some things and removes others. This sieve keeps prime numbers and removes composite numbers.

First, what is a prime?

A prime is a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. A composite has more than two. The number 1 has only one, so it is neither prime nor composite.

1. Keep the smallest unmarked number

Start with 2. It is prime. Keep it, then cross out its multiples from 4 onward. The next unmarked number is 3, so keep 3 and cross out its multiples from 9 onward. Keep going.

2. Start crossing out at the square

For each base prime p, start at p × p. Smaller multiples of p have a smaller prime factor, so an earlier step has already crossed them out. For 5, start at 25: 10, 15, and 20 were removed by 2 or 3.

3. Stop when the next base is too large

When p × p is greater than the upper limit, no more crossing is needed. Every composite up to that limit must have a prime factor at most its square root. All unmarked numbers above 1 are therefore prime.

A range can start above 1

The sieve still uses small base primes such as 2 and 3, even when they are outside the grid. The step card shows the base prime. The counts and prime list include only the visible range.

Select a number to check it

The detail card calculates factors on its own. It can tell you that a number is prime or composite before the sieve reaches it. This does not change the sieve.
